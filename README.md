## Scenario

A small U.S. health care clinic that provides primary-care services experienced a security incident on a Tuesday at about 9:00 a.m. Employees could not access medical records and other files, and a ransom note appeared on their computers. An organized group of unethical hackers, known to target health care and transportation, had encrypted critical files and demanded payment for the decryption key.

The attackers gained access through targeted phishing emails with a malicious attachment. Once downloaded, the attachment installed malware that let the attackers deploy ransomware. The clinic had to shut down computer systems and report the incident.

## Key Findings

- **Initial access:** targeted phishing emails with a malicious attachment.
- **Impact:** ransomware encrypted critical files, including patient records, and halted clinic operations.
- **Threat actors:** an organized group known for targeting health care and transportation; identities unknown.
- **Motive:** financial gain through ransom for a decryption key.
- **Documentation focus:** capturing the 5 W's early supports later investigation and reporting.
- **Open questions:** whether the clinic trains staff on phishing awareness, and whether paying the ransom is wise when decryption is not guaranteed.

## What I Learned

- Clear incident documentation starts as soon as an event is reported, not after everything is solved.
- The 5 W's give a shared picture that other responders and stakeholders can use.
- Health care targets face high pressure to restore patient data quickly, which ransomware groups exploit.
- Phishing awareness and email filtering are common preventive controls after this kind of incident.
- Paying a ransom does not guarantee a working decryption key.
